How I Shipped What Millions of Nike Customers See First

Digital Content Manager (Contract) | Nike App and Nike.com

From June to August 2026 I was a Digital Content Manager at Nike on a planned three-month contract. I published campaign content and app notifications to Nike.com and the Nike app, reaching as many as 20M customers per activation, verified delivery after each send, and co-developed the authoring reference the team now uses to onboard publishers. Other teams decided what each placement should be. My job was getting it live, correct, and on time.

More Requests Than One Calendar Can Hold

Requests reach Nike's publishing team from marketing partners across the business, and one campaign can touch an app shopping surface, a landing page, a product grid, a notification, and the routing between them.

Some arrive incomplete: assets missing, timing that collides with work already scheduled, or a request the platform cannot support. With as many as 20 million people on the other end of one activation, those problems had to be caught before anything was scheduled.

From Request to Live

I owned each placement from brief to verified delivery, and nothing was scheduled until it held up on a real phone. QA sign-off and legal review belonged to others; the build came back to me to schedule, and my part ended once delivery was confirmed.

My Part

Intake the Brief

A brief arrived from a marketing partner with the copy, imagery, links, and timing. I filled in what I could, and when something was missing or conflicting I went to the stakeholder who had the answer while the plan could still change.

  • Assets and copy complete
  • Timing clear of work already scheduled
  • Fits what the platform supports

My Part

Build the Placement

I built it in Nike's proprietary CMS: the module, the copy, the imagery, and the routing behind every tile and button.

  • Matches the Figma source
  • Every tile and button routes to a live destination
  • Alt text, captions, and heading structure in place

My Part

Check It on a Real Device

Before handing it off, I previewed it on a phone and on desktop, across breakpoints, in the app and on the web. Defects went to the development team with what I saw and where.

  • Holds at every breakpoint
  • Behaves the same in the app and on the web
  • Reads at a glance with the sound off
  • Copy and visuals hold to brand guidelines

QA

QA Sign-Off

The build went to QA and came back signed off, or with fixes for me. I ran the same check on other publishers' builds, so I knew what QA would look for before I handed mine off.

Legal

Legal Copy Review

Claims and promotional copy cleared legal review before anything was released.

My Part

Schedule the Release

I scheduled it into a fixed publish window, coordinated with the operations, UX, and technical production teams. A claim that changed after sign-off went back through legal, and a changed link or image went back through the device check, before anything was released.

  • Publish window confirmed
  • No collision with placements already live

My Part

Go Live and Verify

After each send I confirmed the placement myself. Tests on headlines, featured products, and category entry points went into wrap briefs for campaign stakeholders, and those results belong to Nike.

  • Live on Nike.com and in the app
  • Notification delivered
  • Findings reported in the wrap brief

Full Shopping Surfaces

Beyond single placements, I built complete shopping experiences: the hero, the category navigation, the product grid, and the routing that connects them. Where a surface carried SEO metadata and taxonomy tags, I set those in the CMS too.

One August promotional shop had six category entry points, each with its own imagery and menu routing, on one surface with the featured carousel and product grids. I built the pieces one at a time, assembled them in order, and checked that each one led to the right place.

A tile that lands nowhere is a product someone cannot buy.

  • Tennis Slam Product Grid on Nike.comI set up the grid and its filters so the collection loaded the right products and every tile opened a working product page.

  • Nike Soccer x LEGO Collection ShopI assembled the hero, the tabs, and the grid into one surface and checked that each tab filtered to the right products before it went live.

  • Men's Shop Tab with Category NavigationI assembled surfaces like this one component at a time and tested the whole surface before scheduling.

  • Sport Category Page in the Nike AppEach row and menu item had its own destination. I built and checked them in order so no entry point led to an empty page.

  • Jannik Sinner Graphic Tee Product PageEvery placement and grid eventually has to land on a page like this, so I traced each destination through to it before anything was scheduled.

  • Women's Back to School Bags ShopI assembled the tabs, feature card, and grid, then checked it in the app and on mobile web, which do not always render a module the same way.

Learn It, Then Teach It

I joined Nike's publishing team mid-season on a proprietary CMS I had never used, and kept my own notes as I learned it. With a teammate I co-developed the team's authoring reference, and before the contract ended as planned I trained the publishers staying on.

  1. ShadowWatched placements go through before building one.
  2. QAChecked other publishers' builds against Figma, copy, alt text, and links, which taught me what usually breaks.
  3. PublishTook placements from brief to live on my own.
  4. TeachCo-developed the authoring reference and trained the publishers who stayed on.

The reference covers platform constraints, accessibility standards, QA routing, scheduling rules, and how modules differ on mobile and desktop. The team adopted it as the onboarding guide for incoming publishers.
